UnDiffused
Detecting AI-generated images on-device, and the measurements that decided
how.
A complete technical account: what the system does, how every part of it works, and the decision behind
each one, in the order the decisions were actually made.
Akshay Reddy Gujjula
github.com/AkshayReddyGujjula/UnDiffused-AI
Branch stage1-baseline, 3 September 2026
The short version. I shipped a Chrome extension that claimed to detect AI-generated images. Seven months
later I measured it properly and found it detected nothing at all: 0.50 AUROC, zero of fifty generated images
caught. I rebuilt it. The first rebuild scored 0.99 and I threw it away, because I could show it had learned which
dataset an image came from rather than whether it was generated. The version that ships scores 0.954 on
content-matched pairs, abstains on 14% of images, and states a false-positive rate of 6.88% rather than the
5% it was tuned for, because 6.88% is what it measured on data it had never seen.

1. What this is
The claim, the evidence for it, and how to read the numbers.
1.1 The product
UnDiffused is a Chrome extension (Manifest V3). You right-click any image on any page and choose Scan
with UnDiffused. Roughly one second later a panel appears over the page with one of three verdicts: likely
authentic, likely AI generated, or inconclusive. Alongside the verdict sits a forensic toolkit of ten classical
image-analysis tools, and a heatmap showing which regions of the image the model reacted to.
Everything runs on the machine. The neural network is a 24.9 MB ONNX file bundled inside the extension
and executed by onnxruntime-web on WebAssembly. No image, score or verdict is transmitted anywhere.
There is no server, no telemetry and no analytics.

1.3 What AUROC means here, and why the corpus matters more
AUROC is the probability that the model assigns a higher suspicion score to a randomly chosen generated
image than to a randomly chosen real one. 0.5 is a coin flip. 1.0 is perfect. It is rank-based, which has one
consequence that runs through this entire document: AUROC is invariant to any monotonic rescaling of
the scores. Calibration cannot change it, and a good AUROC tells you nothing about whether the
probabilities the user sees are meaningful.
The number is only as honest as the set it is measured on. Every v2 figure here is measured on
content-matched pairs: each real photograph is paired with an AI image generated from that same
photograph's caption. Subject matter is held constant, so the only thing that varies is authenticity. Section 2.4
is the story of why I had to build that, and it is the most important section in this document.

2. How it was built, in order
Ninety-four commits across two phases, seven months apart. The decisions that mattered, and the ones
that were wrong.
The repository has two clearly separated phases. Between 6 and 14 February 2026 I built the extension and
trained models for it. On 2 September 2026 I came back to it, audited it, and found the central claim was false.
Everything after that date is the rebuild.
2.1 Phase one: building the thing (February 2026)
The first phase is 34 commits and produced a working, attractive extension. In order, it added: the extension
scaffold and rename from AI-Image-Checker (0d48b9a, f496a88); a first ONNX model (0a06dac); a heatmap
overlay (5131b69); a high-pass filter and movable windows; several rounds of retraining logged as Epoch 1,
Epoch 2, Epoch 5, Epoch 10; a reverse image search; and then the forensic tools panel with its sliders,
fullscreen, zoom and download controls (983eda6 onward).
Two commits at the end of that phase set up everything that followed. 78c5ecd swapped in a quantized
Swin-Tiny ONNX model, and f4b7ba4 introduced a dual model architecture: a three-class global model and a
two-class local model, their outputs blended 25/75, with an early-exit gate that skipped the local model when
the global score was extreme.
The decision I would take back. Neither model was ever measured. There was no benchmark, no held-out
set, no label map recorded, and no note of which output index meant AI. The code contained a comment
agonising over whether index 0 or index 1 was the AI class. That comment sat there for seven months. It was
arguing about a coin with no faces.
2.2 Phase two begins: the audit (2 September 2026)
I wrote a technical audit of my own project (172ecb2), then revised it twice after criticism (f31676f, 1ff9e89).
The audit predicted one of two outcomes: either the detector worked and I would finally learn which class
index meant AI, or it was inverted and had been calling photographs fake for its whole existence.
Neither happened.
Stage 1: measure the shipped checkpoints (91b562a)
I built a 100-image labelled set: 50 COCO val2017 photographs, chosen because the corpus predates public
diffusion models so authenticity is established by collection date rather than by my judgement, and 50
ELSA_D3 renders spanning four generator families. Then I ran both checkpoints over it and bootstrapped
confidence intervals.

Through the extension's real path the pipeline caught 0 of 50 generated images and flagged 1 of 50
photographs, an accuracy of 0.490 achieved by calling almost everything real. The mechanism was visible in
the score distribution: the final probability was squeezed between 0.221 and 0.655 with a standard deviation
of 0.052, so it almost never crossed the hardcoded 0.5 threshold. The number on screen moved, which made
it look alive, but it moved inside a range that could barely produce a positive verdict.
The decision: rule out my own preprocessing before blaming the model
A chance result is worthless if it comes from feeding the model the wrong input, and there was a specific
reason for suspicion. The tensors are named pixel_values and logits, which is HuggingFace convention,
and HuggingFace's default ViT processor normalises with mean and standard deviation both 0.5 rather than
with ImageNet statistics. The extension used ImageNet statistics.
So I swept 5 normalisations by 2 resize policies by 5 class indices, which is 50 measurements. The strongest
separation found anywhere was AUROC 0.341: that is 0.159 from chance, and in the inverted direction.
Across 50 measurements on 100 images, a maximum deviation of that size is what noise produces. No
preprocessing recovered a detector.
Stage 1b: fix the parsing bug, and report that fixing it did nothing (523f007)
There was a real bug. The output parser handled only the one-class and two-class cases. The three-class
global model fell through to a branch that indexed outputData[i] for batch item i, which is correct only when
there is exactly one class. With three classes and a batch of four quadrants it read flat positions 0, 1, 2, 3: the
first quadrant's three class logits followed by the second quadrant's first logit, mixing values across both the
class and the batch axis, then pushed the result through a sigmoid as though it were a binary logit.
Fixing it moved AUROC from 0.472 to 0.445. Slightly worse. Both numbers are noise and so is their
difference. The bug was concealing an absence, not a capability. I wrote that down as the headline of the
stage-1 note rather than quietly reporting the bug fix as an improvement, because the honest reading is the
whole point: the parsing defect was real and unambiguously wrong, and repairing it recovered nothing,
because there was no signal underneath it to recover.
The same commit introduced contract.ts, which asserts the tensor contract at load time and throws rather
than degrading. That file is now the spine of the project and Section 3.4 covers it in full.
Two corrections to my own audit
Running the code rather than reading it overturned one of my predictions and sharpened another. I had
warned that the early-exit gate would skip the local model and report raw noise; measured early-exit rate was
0.00 across all 100 images, because the buggy global score never left the mid-range that keeps the gate
open. The gate was still bad design, but it was not causing harm, and my audit had overstated it. I also could
not explain the global head's third class; measurement showed it was the argmax for 0 of 50 real and 1 of 50
generated images, with mean probability 0.255 against a uniform 0.333. Not mysterious, just inert.
Stage 2: build the tests that could catch a fake win (c5dfb9b)
Before training a replacement I built the machinery to disbelieve it: a laundering suite (JPEG at three qualities,
double JPEG, WebP, a resize chain, a screenshot simulation, and a 512px q90 normalisation that removes
resolution and compression as a shortcut), a held-out generator split, and a confound control. This ordering
was deliberate. Building the evaluation after seeing the result is how you build an evaluation that agrees with
the result.

3.2 End to end, step by step
1. Menu registration. registerContextMenu (main.ts:146) clears any existing entry and creates
undiffused-scan for image contexts, on both install and startup.
2. The click. handleContextMenuClick (main.ts:106) checks the menu id, that info.srcUrl exists, and that

3. Making sure someone is listening. ensureContentScript (main.ts:44) sends a PING to frame 0. If

only inject on page load, so every tab open before install had none.
4. Dispatch. A SCANNING message goes to frame 0 only (main.ts:121), so an advertising iframe cannot
answer on the page's behalf. A failure here sets a red badge with the reason in its tooltip rather than doing
nothing visible.
5. Buffering. src/content/ready.ts receives the request at module scope, before React has mounted. It
holds a single slot, not a queue: an older queued scan is stale by definition once a newer one arrives.
registerScanHandler drains it synchronously on mount.
6. Getting the pixels. fetchImageViaBackground (imageLoader.ts:4) handles data: and blob: URLs itself.

the worker is not. That asymmetry is the reason Section 7 exists.
7. Saliency and crops, on the main thread. runMultiCropInference (pipeline.ts:89) draws the bitmap to an
OffscreenCanvas, computes a quality map, and picks nine adaptive crops. See 3.3.
8. Transfer. The request gets a UUID, its callbacks go into pendingRequests, a 30 second timeout is armed,
and the bitmap is transferred (zero-copy) to the worker. Scanner.tsx:60 nulls its own reference
immediately, because a transferred bitmap leaves a neutered handle behind that would silently break a
re-scan.
9. The verdict. The worker serialises all work through a promise chain, loads the ONNX session once and
reuses it, then runs one forward pass on the whole image resized to 224x224. That single probability is
the verdict.

a model later shown to carry no signal. The model is trained on whole images resized to 224, so that is what it
is scored on.
3.3 Saliency and adaptive crops
computeQualityMap (saliency.ts) builds a per-pixel importance score from three metrics on the Rec.601 luma,
each min-max normalised across the image and then weighted:
gray     = 0.299R + 0.587G + 0.114B
sharpness= |4-neighbour Laplacian|, averaged over a 5x5 window   weight 0.5
edges    = sqrt(gx^2 + gy^2) from a 3x3 Sobel                    weight 0.3
contrast = sqrt(mean(gray^2) - mean(gray)^2) over 5x5            weight 0.2
quality  = 0.5*sharpness + 0.3*edges + 0.2*contrast
One honest note about that code: the comment calls the first metric the local variance of the Laplacian, and
what is implemented is the local average of its magnitude. The source says so in place, having traded the
exact statistic for speed. It is a proxy for high-frequency energy either way.
getAdaptiveCrops then slides a 224x224 window at stride 56, scores each candidate by sampling the quality
map every 8 pixels (784 samples per window), sorts by score, and greedily selects nine while rejecting any
candidate overlapping an already-selected crop by more than 30% of a crop's area. If fewer than nine survive,
the 3x3 grid fills the remainder. Deep scan mode skips saliency entirely and tiles the image.
3.4 contract.ts, the spine
This file exists because the shipped inference path guessed at the model's interface and was wrong about it
for the project's entire history. Its opening comment states the thesis of the whole project:
That is the defining hazard of ML inference code: wrong indices still produce finite floats, a sigmoid still returns
something in [0,1], and a progress bar will happily render it. The only defences are asserting the tensor
contract at load time and testing against images whose labels are known.
What it enforces:
 Tensor names and arity, at load time. assertModelContract checks that the session exposes exactly the
declared input and output and exactly one of each, and throws rather than degrading.
 Output shape, on first inference, because the batch dimension is symbolic until then.
 Striding. logitsToDistributions indexes by i * classCount, never by i. That single change is the v1 bug
fix, and a test pins it with four batch items whose dominant classes differ so an indexing error cannot pass
by coincidence.

3.5 The forensic toolkit
Ten classical tools sit alongside the neural verdict. They are deterministic, explainable, and were largely
written in phase one. None of them feeds the verdict; they are there so a user can look at the evidence
themselves rather than trusting a number.
Tool
What it computes
ELA
Re-encodes to JPEG at a chosen quality, decodes, and maps the per-pixel absolute residual. Normalised
against the 95th percentile rather than the max, so one bright splice boundary cannot wash out the rest of
the image.
FFT
Hand-written radix-2 Cooley-Tukey FFT over rows then columns of a 256x256 grayscale, log-magnitude,
DC-centred, plus a detector for symmetric peaks that indicate upsampling grid artifacts.
PRNU
Multiplicative sensor-noise residual: Gaussian denoise, then (image - denoised) / image. Scored by the
coefficient of variation of 32x32 block variances and by Pearson correlation between Sobel edge strength
and residual magnitude, which measures how much scene content leaked into the residual.
Clone
Copy-move detection. 4x4 mean-pooled L2-normalised luminance descriptors, FNV-1a hashed into
buckets, then cosine similarity within buckets. Because descriptors are already normalised, the cosine is
a plain dot product.
Aberration
Per-channel Sobel at high-contrast edges, measuring the angular separation between the red/green and
blue/green gradient vectors. Real lenses refract the channels differently, so low separation is the synthetic
signal.
Noise
9-point Laplacian residual, per-block variance, coefficient of variation inverted into a uniformity score.
Suspiciously uniform noise is the tell, which is the opposite of natural sensor behaviour.
Gradient
Sobel, Laplacian, or a full Canny pipeline with non-maximum suppression over four angle bins and
iterative 8-connectivity hysteresis, written without recursion to stay inside the JavaScript call stack.
Compression
8x8 DCT per block, then a brute-force search over JPEG quality levels for the quantiser that best explains
the coefficients. A block re-saved at a different quality misfits, which is the cross-compression signal.
Metadata
A hand-rolled JPEG marker walker: APP1 for EXIF and XMP, APP13 for IPTC, a TIFF IFD reader
handling both byte orders and following the ExifIFD and GPS pointer tags. No third-party library. Software
strings are matched against an AI generator keyword list.
Highlight
Finds specular highlights, fits a luminance gradient over a 21x21 patch to get a light direction, and
compares angles with a wraparound-correct circular difference.
All ten run in the page, on canvas, with no network access.

4. The model
Architecture, training, export, quantization, calibration, and the one decision that kept a better model off
the shelf for a day.
4.1 Architecture
input   pixel_values   float32 [batch, 3, 224, 224]
        normalised with ImageNet mean/std, bicubic resize
trunk   facebook/dinov2-small (ViT-S/14), last blocks unfrozen
pool    mean over patch tokens, excluding CLS
head    Linear(384 -> 1), then .squeeze(-1)
output  logits         float32 [batch]
        P(AI) = sigmoid(logit / 1.9077)
One model, one logit, one forward pass. There is no class-index question to get wrong, which was a
deliberate response to the v1 failure. The head is trained with binary cross-entropy against label 1 =
generated, so a higher logit means more likely AI as a property of the training objective rather than as an
assumption written in a comment.
DINOv2 was chosen because it is self-supervised. It never saw the labels real or generated during
pre-training, so it cannot have learned this task in advance, and its features are known to be strong for linear
probing. Mean pooling over patch tokens beat CLS and beat CLS concatenated with mean, measured rather
than assumed: 0.9748 against 0.967 and 0.982 on the in-distribution test split, but critically all three collapsed
to 0.64 to 0.66 on content-matched pairs, which is how the shortcut was first quantified across
representations.
4.2 Why a frozen probe first
The first v2 was a frozen backbone with a linear probe on cached features, trainable in minutes on a CPU.
That was a deliberate ordering: a probe measures how much of the task is already linearly separable in the
backbone's features, and it makes the experiment loop fast enough to run the corpus experiments that
exposed the shortcut. Fine-tuning came only after the data question was settled, so that the GPU was spent
on a corpus known to be sound.

4.4 Export, and the operator that does not exist in a browser
Export runs the ONNX graph and the PyTorch model on identical input and refuses to write the file if they
disagree beyond 1e-3. Measured divergence was 1.3e-05. It then quantizes to int8, which takes the file from
88 MB to 24.9 MB, a compression of 3.55x.
Quantization is lossy, so it is measured rather than assumed: the exporter reports the maximum divergence
against PyTorch (1.37 on probe tensors) and warns that the int8 file must be re-scored on the benchmark
before it ships. It is, and the int8 model is the only one whose numbers appear in this document.
The operator constraint is the important part:
quantize_dynamic(fp32, int8, weight_type=QuantType.QInt8,
                 op_types_to_quantize=["MatMul"])   # NOT Conv
Left to itself, quantize_dynamic also quantizes Conv, producing ConvInteger. The WASM backend of
onnxruntime-web implements MatMulInteger and does not implement ConvInteger, so the model loads
perfectly in Python and cannot load in a browser at all. Both exporters now call assert_browser_compatible
and refuse to write a graph containing an operator the target runtime lacks. The cost of leaving the
patch-embedding Conv in fp32 is 0.7 MB.
4.5 Metadata generated from the artifact, never typed
Every field in detector_v2_finetuned_meta.json is read off the exported graph. The previous attempt at this
declared the input as "input" and the output as "output" when the real tensors were pixel_values and
logits. Hand-written metadata describes what someone believed; generated metadata describes what
exists.
4.6 Calibration
The fine-tune's raw sigmoid was overconfident, with an expected calibration error of 0.061. Temperature
scaling fits a single scalar T minimising negative log-likelihood on held-out data and reports sigmoid(logit /
T). T = 1.9077, greater than 1, which shrinks every logit toward zero. That is the expected direction for a
fine-tuned network against a linear probe.

The two v2 models disagree about where calibration belongs. The frozen probe folds its temperature into the
exported linear layer, so its graph emits an already-calibrated logit. The fine-tune does not, and is scaled at
read time. A bare divisor inside the parser would therefore double-apply the moment anyone repointed at the
probe, and would do it silently, because the result stays inside [0,1] and still renders. So the temperature is a
field on the model contract, DETECTOR_V2_PROBE declares 1.0, and a test pins the arrangement.
4.7 The abstention band, and the number I chose to publish
Three states, never a bare percentage. Below 0.2102 is likely authentic, above 0.6482 is likely AI generated,
and in between the extension says inconclusive rather than guessing. The band is fitted to a 5% false-positive
target under a 25% abstention ceiling, both fixed in advance. It is tuned to false-positive rate rather than to
accuracy because wrongly calling a genuine photograph fake is the more damaging error.
The band was fitted to hit 5% and delivers 6.88% on data it has never seen. The extension publishes
the 6.88%.
Quoting the 4.97% would be reporting a fitting-set number as a deployment number, which is a miniature of
the exact failure this whole project documents. Re-fitting the band on the external set would recover the
advertised 5%, and I rejected that: it would consume the only clean measurement of the shipped thresholds in
exchange for a nicer-sounding number. The validation fit is kept in a fittedOnVal sub-object as provenance,
and a test asserts that the published FPR is the larger of the two, so nobody can quietly swap them back.
4.8 Failing loudly
A late review found that a non-finite logit still produced a verdict. sigmoid(Infinity) is exactly 1 and
sigmoid(-Infinity) is exactly 0, so an overflowed output rendered as maximum confidence. NaN was
worse: every comparison in toVerdict is false against NaN, so it fell through to inconclusive while the result
still carried status ok.
logit       P(AI)     verdict            status
NaN         NaN       inconclusive       ok      <- fabricated
Infinity    1         likely_ai          ok      <- fabricated
-Infinity   0         likely_authentic   ok      <- fabricated
The parser now throws. Because that throw must not cost a good verdict, the per-crop evidence loop catches
and continues, so a single bad heatmap tile cannot discard a whole-image result that already succeeded.

7. Security model
One component holds real privilege. This is what it can do and what stops it.
7.1 The threat
Inference is entirely local, so the interesting attack surface is not the model. It is the service worker. It holds
host permissions for every http(s) origin so it can fetch an image the page will not hand over directly, which
means its fetch is bound by neither CORS nor the page's own origin. The URL it fetches is whatever image
the user right-clicked, and a page controls that completely.
That is a server-side request forgery primitive with a human in the loop. A page embeds an image pointing at
the loopback interface, the local network, or a cloud instance-metadata endpoint; one right-click fetches it with
the extension's privileges and returns the bytes into the page's own context.
7.2 What the guard does
The original check validated the protocol and nothing else. The current one, in src/background/urlGuard.ts,
refuses loopback, RFC1918 private ranges, link-local including 169.254.169.254, CGNAT, the unspecified
address, .localhost and .internal suffixes, and the IPv6 unique-local and link-local ranges.
Getting this right took three attempts, and each failure is now a test.
(1) A regex over dotted-decimal blocked 127.0.0.1 and allowed 2130706433, which is the same address
written as a 32-bit integer.
(2) That same regex prefix-matched hostnames, so 10.example.com, an ordinary public domain, was refused.
(3) Enumerating IPv6 forms individually allowed ::ffff:7f00:1, which is loopback written with hex hextets:
the check looked for an embedded dotted quad and found no dot.
The address is now parsed properly, in decimal, octal, hex and short forms, and range-checked numerically.
The whole :: prefix is refused, because none of 0000::/8 is globally routable.
7.3 Redirects, and the residual risk
Validating the requested URL is not enough while fetch follows redirects: a permitted public host can answer
302 with a Location inside the blocked ranges. A service worker cannot inspect the hops, because redirect:
'manual' yields an opaque response with no readable header. So the final response.url is re-checked before
the body is read.
The residual is written next to the check rather than left implied: the request is still issued, so a blind GET to
an internal address remains possible. What is prevented is the response coming back.

8. Limitations, and what I would do next
Stated plainly, because someone will ask, and because the ones I found myself are the point.
8.1 What this does not do
Reliable universal detection of AI-generated images is an open research problem and this does not solve it.
Published detectors routinely lose more than twenty points moving from curated benchmarks to real-world
images. Specifically:
 Four generator families, all open-source diffusion. No Midjourney, no Firefly, no autoregressive
models. Cross-family generalisation beyond diffusion is untested, and this is the single most reproduced
weakness in the literature. I have observed poor detection on images from at least one commercial
generator, which is exactly this gap.
 Two source corpora. COCO and ELSA_D3 over LAION.
 The real half of the matched set is LAION web imagery, which includes graphics, product renders and
screenshots rather than clean photography. Label noise depresses the score, so this makes the control
conservative rather than inflated, but it is not ideal.
 A 14% abstention rate is a real cost. About one image in seven gets no answer, and the measured
false-positive rate on those it does rule on is 6.88%, not the 5% the band was fitted to.
 0.7 to 0.9 seconds per image on single-threaded WASM, plus a one-off session initialisation of about 1.4
seconds warm and up to 30 seconds on a cold cache while the 24.9 MB graph is fetched and optimised.
 No provenance verification. Where C2PA metadata exists it should be read deterministically rather than
estimated.
8.2 Known weaknesses in the repository itself
 Split construction is duplicated in four scripts. finetune_gpu.py and calibrate_onnx.py agree,
deliberately and with a comment saying they must. But train_matched_probe.py and
export_probe_onnx.py use a different random source and a different seed, so the probe and the fine-tune
were selected on different partitions. Both were scored externally on the same held-out set, so the
comparison in this document stands, but the duplication is a maintenance trap and should be one imported
function.
 Temperature fitting and band selection exist in three copies across the bench scripts, and they have
already drifted into different numerical paths that compute the same scalar.
 Several scripts resolve paths relative to the working directory, and normalization_probe.py only runs
from scripts/bench.
 The resize interpolation mode is not in the model metadata. Training and scoring both use bicubic and
agree, but nothing pins it, so a future caller choosing bilinear would silently degrade the numbers.
 corpus_v1 does not exclude matched_control_v1 offsets, so those two could overlap. It does not touch
any shipped figure, because the shipped model trains on matched_corpus_v1 and that is verifiably disjoint,
but the older probe experiments carry the caveat.
8.3 What I would do next, in order of value
1. C2PA provenance. The highest-value addition by a distance, because it is deterministic and cannot be
wrong where a signature exists. The metadata tool already parses JPEG APP1 and APP13 by hand, so the
parsing foundation is there. A detector that reads a signature when one is present and estimates only when
none is beats an estimator everywhere.

2. More generator families. Midjourney and Firefly samples would materially strengthen or materially
weaken the claim, and either outcome is worth having. This is the largest open question about the model.
3. Deduplicate the split and calibration logic into one imported module, and pin the interpolation mode
into the metadata contract where the load-time assertion can check it.
4. Trim the package. The four WASM builds are 40 MB and only one is used on any given machine.
5. Drop the declarative content script. On-demand injection now works, so the extension could stop
injecting into every page unconditionally and narrow its permissions further.
8.4 The thing worth taking away
Four times in this project a number looked right and was not, and every single time the failure was silent: a
parser reading across array boundaries still returned a float; a model that had learned a dataset seam still
scored 0.990 on a held-out generator; an int8 export that could not load in a browser passed every Python
check; a NaN logit still produced a verdict with status ok.
Nothing threw. Nothing logged. A progress bar rendered every one of them.
The defence is not better code. It is measurement against known labels, and a contract that refuses
rather than degrades. The most valuable number this project produced was 0.884, chosen over a 0.990 I
could not defend.
